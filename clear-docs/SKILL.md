# Clear documentation

Help the reader finish their task. Put the answer or action first, then the detail needed to use it correctly.

## Find the job

Choose the page's purpose: learn something, perform an action, look up a contract, or understand a decision. Separate purposes when mixing them makes the reader hunt. A small product may need only a README and a few guides.

Use source code, actual controls and observed results to establish current behavior. Earlier investigations explain why something changed; they are not proof of today's behavior. Keep historical evidence outside ordinary navigation.

An authorized rewrite can proceed without outline approval. Ask only when a missing audience, factual source or consequential scope decision would change the work.

## Write for someone scanning

- Lead with what the reader can do, what happens, or the answer they need.
- Use short paragraphs and descriptive headings. Give each section a useful stopping point.
- Vary sentence length. Follow a dense explanation with a short, concrete sentence.
- Prefer a familiar verb over an abstract noun. Name the thing that failed and the next useful action.
- Use steps for sequences, lists for parallel choices and tables for comparisons. Ordinary explanations can stay prose.
- Keep a condition or consequence beside the action it affects. Put optional mechanics in a linked reference.
- Remove repeated introductions, ceremonial conclusions and status reports disguised as instructions.

Do not solve density by turning every sentence into a bullet. A page should have rhythm and a clear hierarchy, not a second wall made of headings.

Apply the owner's writing preferences. Avoid em dashes, inflated language and invented technical labels. Read [examples.md](references/examples.md) when the text is technically correct but still hard to understand.

## Choose what belongs

Keep one current source for each setting, procedure and policy. Link to it from related pages. Defaults belong beside the decision they help, rather than repeated throughout the docs.

Distinguish implemented behavior, verified behavior and requirements still missing. State a limit where it changes the reader's next step. Avoid repeating a general disclaimer in every section.

For a product README, lead with the product, its primary download or starting action, and one current screenshot of the main experience. Use consistent, centered image widths. Keep feature summaries short and link to focused guides.

For diagrams, choose a visual form that fits the question. A timeline, nested map, evidence graph and comparison need different compositions. Keep an established theme, but make relationships and hierarchy carry meaning. Avoid turning every subject into the same boxes and arrows.

For a substantial documentation refresh, consider chapter banners that help readers recognize the subject. Match the product's visual language and give different topics distinct motifs. Keep headings as selectable text, artwork secondary and the next action visible. A banner belongs at an entry or chapter opening, not above every section.

For a requested PDF or manual, read [manuals.md](references/manuals.md). Do not generate a PDF merely because Markdown guides exist.

## Verify the result

Read the first screen as a newcomer. Can they identify the next action without reading the whole page?

Check commands, UI labels, local links and diagram facts against the implementation. Render the README and representative long and short pages at normal width. Inspect screenshots and diagrams at their displayed size, including narrow layouts.

Review the full collection for duplicated explanations, abandoned pages and misleading navigation. End with current instructions, explicit verification limits and a clean path to deeper detail.
